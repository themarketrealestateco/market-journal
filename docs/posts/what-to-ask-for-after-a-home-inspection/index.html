What Can You Ask For After a Home Inspection? Repairs, Credits, or a Lower Price

By Steven Smith · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read

The inspection report came back, and it is twenty pages long. Now what? Whether you are buying in Baton Rouge, on the Gulf Coast of Alabama, or anywhere else, the answer is the same: if your contract has an inspection contingency, you can usually ask the seller to make repairs, give you a credit at closing, or lower the price. You can also walk away if the problems are serious enough and you are still inside your contingency window.

Here is how those options work and how to decide which one to ask for.

What are your options after an inspection?

Zillow says buyers generally have three main options: ask for repairs, request a price reduction, or ask for a credit at closing. Redfin lists the same three and adds a fourth, an escrow repair agreement, which it calls a last resort. Opendoor adds a request for a home warranty as another path.

  • Seller repairs. The seller fixes specific items before closing. Opendoor suggests asking for licensed contractors and receipts for the work.
  • Credit at closing. Redfin explains that a credit appears on the closing statement as a lump sum, and that if you are financing, your lender has to approve it. Zillow notes it can cover repair costs without changing the purchase price.
  • Price reduction. This lowers the purchase price, and with it your loan amount, but it does not put cash in your hands at closing.
  • Home warranty. Opendoor says a warranty covers major systems and appliances for the first year.

Which one should you ask for?

A few simple questions help:

  1. Is it a safety or structural issue? Repairs done before closing, with proof, can give you peace of mind on things like electrical, roofing, or active leaks.
  2. Do you want to choose your own contractor? A credit or price reduction lets you hire who you want and schedule the work on your timeline.
  3. How is your financing set up? Credits and price changes interact with your loan and your appraisal, so talk with your lender and your agent before you ask. Our post on what happens if your appraisal comes in low explains why price changes matter.
  4. How much cash do you need to close? A credit may be limited by your loan terms. Our guide to closing costs for buyers covers what is already in that number.

What should you focus on, and what should you let go?

Opendoor advises focusing on safety, structural, and major system issues, such as the roof, HVAC, electrical, and plumbing, rather than cosmetic items. That matches how most negotiations stay productive. Chipped paint, worn caulk, and a loose cabinet knob are normal for a home that has been lived in. A failing roof or a plumbing leak is not.

A long list also tends to get a worse response than a short, clear one. Pick the items that matter most, get a written estimate from a licensed contractor for anything expensive, and attach it to your request.

How long do you have?

Your contract sets the deadline, and it varies. Redfin says the window is usually 5 to 10 days after the inspection, and Opendoor says repair requests typically fall within 7 to 10 business days. Do not rely on either number. Check your own contract, and ask your agent for the exact date.

Redfin notes that if you are still within your timeline, you can back out without losing your earnest money. Whether that applies to you depends on the contingencies in your contract, which is a good reason to read them before you make an offer, not after.

Put every agreement in writing

A verbal "sure, we'll fix that" is not enough. Any repair, credit, or price change should be written into a signed amendment to the contract, with the item, the amount, and the date it must be completed. If repairs are being made, ask for a final walk-through before closing so you can see them yourself.

A note on sources

Two of the sources above, Redfin (published July 31, 2025, modified September 2025) and Zillow (September 17, 2025), are more than 12 months old. We paired them with Opendoor's guide, updated February 5, 2026. All three describe the same basic options. Contract terms and deadlines vary, so rely on your own contract.

The takeaway

After an inspection, decide which problems truly matter, ask for a repair, a credit, or a price reduction in writing, and watch your contingency deadline. A short, well-documented request usually works better than a long list.
