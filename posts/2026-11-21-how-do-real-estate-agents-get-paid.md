Real estate agents are paid a commission that is negotiated and agreed to in writing. In most cases the agent is paid at closing, and generally only if the sale closes. Who pays that commission, and how much it is, can be negotiated, and the rules around it changed in August 2024.

Here is how it works in plain terms, so you can ask the right questions before you sign anything.

## How does the agent actually get paid?

According to [Redfin](https://www.redfin.com/blog/how-do-real-estate-agents-get-paid/), agents are paid through a negotiated commission agreed to in writing, typically after closing. The title or escrow company releases the commission at the closing table, and the agent's brokerage then splits it with the agent. The split varies from one brokerage to another.

If a deal does not close, agents generally don't get paid for the work they put in. That is worth knowing, because it means an agent can spend weeks showing homes or marketing a listing and receive nothing if the sale falls apart.

## Is the commission a set rate?

No. There is no standard or fixed rate. [Florida Realtors](https://www.floridarealtors.org/law-ethics/nar-settlement-faqs), summarizing the National Association of Realtors settlement rules, says compensation is "fully negotiable and not set by law." Anyone who tells you the rate is fixed is not giving you the full picture.

Under the rules, compensation also has to be clearly stated in the agreement, not left open-ended. You should be able to read your agreement and know how the agent will be paid.

## What changed in August 2024?

Two changes matter most for consumers.

**Compensation can no longer be listed in the MLS.** Florida Realtors notes that, effective August 2024, compensation information of any kind can no longer be placed in the MLS (the multiple listing service where agents share listings). Offers of buyer-agent compensation are now made outside the MLS, directly between the parties.

**Buyers need a written agreement before touring.** The same source quotes NAR: "Written buyer agreements are required before a buyer tours a home." This is a rule for agents who are working with buyers, and it means the conversation about pay now happens up front, not at the end.

## Who pays the buyer's agent now?

It depends on what is negotiated. Sellers can still choose to offer compensation to a buyer's agent, and Florida Realtors says they may do so "in the amount of their choice." No law requires a seller to make that offer.

Buyers can also ask the seller to cover their agent's fee as part of an offer, as [Redfin](https://www.redfin.com/blog/how-do-real-estate-agents-get-paid/) points out. The seller may agree, counter, or decline. That means the buyer should know ahead of time what they owe their own agent if the seller does not pay all of it.

## What should I ask before I sign an agreement?

- **How much am I paying, and how is it calculated?** A percentage, a flat fee, or something else.
- **Who is expected to pay, and what happens if the seller offers less?** Get this in writing before you make an offer.
- **What services do I get for that fee?** Ask about showings, negotiation, paperwork, and communication.
- **How long does the agreement last, and can I end it?** Know the term and what happens if the relationship isn't working.
- **What happens if the deal does not close?** Understand what, if anything, you owe.

If you are selling, ask the same questions about the listing agreement, including whether you plan to offer compensation to a buyer's agent and how that will be marketed.

## The takeaway

Commissions are negotiable, agreed in writing, and usually paid at closing. Before you tour a home or sign a listing agreement, ask your agent to walk you through exactly how they are paid, who pays, and what you receive in return. A good agent will welcome the question and answer it clearly.
