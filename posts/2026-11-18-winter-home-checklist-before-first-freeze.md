Before the first hard freeze, do four things: protect exposed pipes, test your smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, have your heating system inspected, and seal the air leaks you can find. None of it is expensive, and each one prevents a repair that is.

We walk through a lot of homes in this business, and winter problems almost always trace back to something small that was skipped in November. Here is the checklist we would use on our own house, built from guidance published by the [American Red Cross](https://www.redcross.org/get-help/how-to-prepare-for-emergencies/types-of-emergencies/winter-storm/frozen-pipes.html), the [U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2026/Keep-Warm-and-Safe-This-Winter-Tips-for-Using-Generators-Furnaces-and-Space-Heaters), and the [U.S. Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/femp/home-energy-checklist).

## How do I keep pipes from freezing?

Frozen pipes can burst and flood a home, even in milder climates where a hard freeze is rare and plumbing is less prepared for it. The Red Cross recommends these steps:

- **Disconnect outdoor hoses.** Remove, drain, and store them. Close the indoor valves that supply outdoor hose faucets, then open the outdoor faucets so any water left in the line has room to expand.
- **Insulate exposed pipes.** Focus on attics, basements, crawl spaces, garages, and the hot and cold lines under kitchen and bathroom cabinets. The Red Cross notes that even a quarter inch of newspaper offers meaningful protection.
- **Keep the garage door closed** if water lines run through the garage.
- **Open cabinet doors** under sinks on exterior walls so warm air reaches the pipes.
- **Let a faucet drip** during very cold weather, on lines that run through exposed areas.
- **Keep the thermostat steady** day and night, and if you travel, leave the heat on at no lower than 55°F.

If water slows to a trickle, suspect a frozen pipe. Keep the faucet open, apply gentle heat with a hair dryer or heating pad, and never use an open flame. If you can't find or thaw the frozen section, call a licensed plumber.

## Are my smoke and carbon monoxide alarms ready?

This is the five-minute task most people put off. The CPSC advises:

- Smoke alarms on every level and inside each bedroom.
- Carbon monoxide alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas.
- Testing alarms monthly and replacing batteries at least once a year, unless you have sealed 10-year battery alarms.

Do it this weekend, and write the date on a calendar so you remember next time.

## Has my heating system been inspected?

The CPSC recommends a professional inspection of fuel-burning heating equipment every year. That includes furnaces, boilers, fireplaces, wood stoves, water heaters, chimneys, flues, and vents. Schedule it now, before the first cold snap, when technicians are busiest and an unexpected failure is hardest to fix.

Also swap or clean your furnace filter. The Department of Energy lists filter care as a routine task for furnaces and heat pumps.

## What about space heaters and generators?

If you use either one this winter, the rules are simple:

- Keep portable heaters at least three feet from curtains, furniture, and bedding.
- Plug electric space heaters directly into a wall outlet, not a power strip or extension cord, and turn them off when you sleep.
- Run portable generators only outdoors, at least 20 feet from the house, with the exhaust pointed away from buildings. Never run one in a garage, basement, or shed.

The CPSC reports that portable heaters are involved in an average of 1,600 fires a year, and that about 750 people died from generator-related carbon monoxide poisoning between 2012 and 2022. Both numbers are a good reason to follow the rules above.

## Where is my home losing heat?

Small gaps add up. The Department of Energy says a typical home loses more than 25% of its heat through windows and that small cracks and holes can add up to as much air loss as an open window or door. Some low-cost fixes it suggests:

- Add weatherstripping or storm windows to leaky windows, or use rope caulk or window film as a quick fix.
- Seal the largest air leaks first, which are often around plumbing penetrations, chimneys, and recessed lights.
- Insulate heating ducts that run through unheated attics or crawl spaces.
- Set your water heater to the "warm" setting, around 120°F, and check for an insulating blanket.
- Use a programmable thermostat to lower the temperature automatically at night.

## Why this matters if you plan to sell

A home that has been maintained is easier to sell. Buyers and inspectors notice water stains, old caulk, and neglected systems. Keeping a simple record of your seasonal maintenance gives you something concrete to point to when the time comes.

## The takeaway

Pick one Saturday before the first freeze. Disconnect the hoses, insulate what you can reach, test every alarm, book the heating inspection, and seal the obvious drafts. It takes a few hours and can save you from a burst pipe or a failed furnace in the middle of January. For anything involving gas, electrical work, or your insurance coverage, check with a licensed professional.
